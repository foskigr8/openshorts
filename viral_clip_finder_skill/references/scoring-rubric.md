# Scoring Rubric — 8-Axis Anchor Descriptors

This is the deep reference for the 8-axis scoring rubric. Read this when scoring borderline candidates, or when you want to verify you're applying the rubric consistently.

The skill supports TWO rubric variants:
- **Tier S (Short-form, 15–30s):** the original 8-axis rubric, max 100 points. Default for TikTok/Reels/Shorts/X.
- **Tier L (Long-form narrative, 60–180s):** a modified rubric with shifted weights and a new Narrative Arc axis, max 100 points. For YouTube long-form, X long video, LinkedIn, multi-part series.

Each axis has:
- **Definition** — what the axis measures
- **Why it matters** — the underlying logic
- **Tier S anchor descriptors** — what 0, 5, 10, and max score look like for short-form
- **Tier L anchor descriptors** — what 0, 5, 10, and max score look like for long-form (only when weights differ)
- **Common scoring errors** — how to avoid inflating or deflating

- 85–100: Tier A (flagship)
- 70–84: Tier B (solid)
- 55–69: Tier C (needs editing work)
- Below 55: Drop

## 1. Hook Strength

**Definition:** Does the opening 1.5 seconds demand attention? Is there a pattern interrupt, curiosity gap, or open loop in the very first sentence?

**Why it matters:** On short-form platforms, the algorithm weights the first 3 seconds of watch time most heavily. If the viewer doesn't stay past second 3, the clip dies regardless of how good the payoff is. Hook strength is the single most predictive axis of virality.

**Anchor descriptors:**

- **15 (max):** The opening 1.5 seconds contain a clear pattern interrupt, curiosity gap, or open loop that forces the viewer to keep watching. The hook is also *specific* — not just "this will surprise you" but a specific claim or scenario that demands resolution.
  - Example: "You're not busy. You're just bad at prioritizing." (Pattern interrupt + specific claim in 8 words.)

- **12:** Strong hook with one of the three patterns (interrupt / curiosity / loop), but slightly less specific or slightly slower to land.
  - Example: "Here's the question every millionaire asks themselves every morning." (Curiosity gap, but takes 3 seconds to set up.)

- **10:** Solid hook that grabs attention but isn't exceptional. Has at least one engaging element but lacks the snap of a 12+.
  - Example: "Most people waste their mornings without realizing it." (Mild pattern interrupt, no specifics yet.)

- **7:** Mediocre hook. Doesn't actively lose the viewer but doesn't grab them. Often a generic statement that the rest of the clip has to overcome.
  - Example: "Today I want to talk about productivity." (No hook, just topic introduction.)

- **5:** Weak hook. Likely to lose 30%+ of viewers in the first 3 seconds. Often starts with "So," "I think," "You know," or restates the question.
  - Example: "So, I was thinking about this earlier, and I think productivity is actually really interesting." (Throat-clearing, no hook.)

- **0:** Hostile hook. Actively repels viewers. Often long setup, abstract conceptual opening, or topic introduction without stakes.
  - Example: "Before we get into the main topic, I want to provide some context that will help you understand where I'm coming from." (Anti-hook.)

**Common scoring errors:**
- Inflating because the rest of the clip is great. Hook strength is independent of payoff. A great clip with a weak hook is a Tier B, not a Tier A.
- Inflating because the speaker is charismatic. Charisma helps but doesn't replace a structural hook.
- Deflating because the hook is "too aggressive." Pattern interrupts often feel aggressive — that's the point. Don't punish what works.

## 2. Payoff Density

**Definition:** Insight, punchline, or specificity per second. Does the clip land hard and fast, or is it slow and meandering?

**Why it matters:** Density drives rewatch value and shareability. A 25-second clip with 5 distinct insights will be rewatched; a 25-second clip with 1 insight and 24 seconds of elaboration will not. Density is the second most predictive axis of virality.

**Anchor descriptors:**

- **15 (max):** Every sentence delivers a distinct insight, punchline, or specific. There's no filler. The clip rewards rewatching because the viewer can't absorb it all in one pass.
  - Example: "Stop networking. Start being useful. The people you want to meet don't need another contact. They need someone who's already done the work. So pick three people. Build something for each of them. Send it. Don't ask for anything. Six months of this and your network will be unrecognizable."
  - 8 distinct moves in 25 seconds: reframe → diagnose → tactic → tactic → tactic → tactic → outcome timeline → outcome timeline.

- **12:** High density with 3–4 distinct moves in 25 seconds. Solid clips that deliver multiple insights but have moments of elaboration.
  - Example: A clip that delivers a reframe, a tactic, and an outcome, with brief connective tissue.

- **10:** Moderate density. One strong insight with supporting context, or 2 insights with more elaboration. The clip is solid but doesn't reward rewatching.
  - Example: "The reason founders fail isn't bad ideas. It's bad timing. Most founders build for a market that isn't ready yet. The best founders I know wait — sometimes 18 months — for the market to catch up before they ship."

- **7:** Low density. One insight with significant elaboration, or setup-heavy with thin payoff.
  - Example: A clip that takes 20 seconds of setup to deliver a 5-second insight.

- **5:** Minimal density. Mostly setup, very thin payoff. The clip is closer to "a person talking" than "a clip."
  - Example: A 25-second clip with one quotable line and 22 seconds of context.

- **0:** No payoff. The clip is all setup, no insight, no punchline. Often a meandering story or conceptual discussion that doesn't resolve.

**Common scoring errors:**
- Inflating because the speaker is articulate. Articulation is not density — a beautifully-spoken platitude is still a platitude.
- Deflating because the clip is "too dense." Density is the goal, not a flaw. Don't punish clarity.
- Confusing specificity for density. Specifics contribute to density, but a clip can be specific without being dense (one specific scenario in 25 seconds) or dense without being specific (abstract ideas stacked quickly).

## 3. Standalone Clarity

**Definition:** Can a stranger with zero context follow this clip cold? Does it require having seen earlier parts of the source material?

**Why it matters:** Viral clips reach cold audiences — strangers who've never seen the source podcast/stream/video. If the clip requires context, the cold viewer is confused and scrolls. Standalone clarity is non-negotiable for virality.

**Anchor descriptors:**

- **15 (max):** The clip is fully self-contained. A stranger can understand every reference, every pronoun, every claim without any prior context. No "she," "he," "they," "this thing," "what we were just talking about" without clear referent.
  - Example: "I worked with a founder last year — 32 years old, Series B, $14M raised, 60 employees. He was working 80-hour weeks and his company was dying. Not because he wasn't smart. Because he was spending 47% of his calendar on meetings about meetings."
  - Every reference is grounded. No prior context needed.

- **12:** Mostly self-contained. One or two minor references that a stranger can infer from context, but no major dependencies.
  - Example: A clip that mentions "the deck" without explaining what deck — but the surrounding context makes it clear it's a pitch deck for fundraising.

- **10:** Requires light context that the viewer can supply from general knowledge. Often industry-context references (e.g. "the Series B crunch" — assumes startup knowledge but not specific source knowledge).
  - Example: "When you're scaling past 50 employees, the founder-CEO transition is brutal." (Requires knowing what founder-CEO transition means, but not knowing the specific founder.)

- **7:** Requires significant context. The clip makes sense to existing audience but cold viewers will feel excluded.
  - Example: "After what happened last quarter with Sarah's team, I think we need to fundamentally rethink our approach." (Requires knowing what happened, who Sarah is, what the approach was.)

- **5:** Heavily context-dependent. The clip makes almost no sense cold.
  - Example: "And that's why the launch failed — same pattern as before, same root cause." (Both "the launch" and "before" are unspecified.)

- **0:** The clip is incomprehensible without prior context. Often an in-joke, a callback, or a continuation of a longer story.
  - Example: "So yeah, basically what you said, but with the opposite conclusion." (What did they say? What's the conclusion? Cold viewer has no idea.)

**Common scoring errors:**
- Inflating because the clip is clear to *you* — you've read the surrounding transcript. The cold viewer hasn't. Score as if you've seen nothing else.
- Deflating because the clip uses industry terminology. Industry terms are fine if the audience for that niche would understand them. Don't penalize a business clip for saying "Series B" — that's standard vocab for the business niche.
- Failing to check pronoun referents. "He," "she," "they," "this" — every pronoun needs a clear referent within the clip itself.

## 4. Emotional Spike

**Definition:** Does the clip produce a real, identifiable emotion in the viewer? Laughter, anger, awe, validation, anxiety, relief, schadenfreude, inspiration.

**Why it matters:** Emotion drives engagement behaviors — comments, shares, saves. A clip that produces no emotion is forgotten. A clip that produces a strong emotion is acted on.

**Anchor descriptors:**

- **10 (max):** The clip produces a sharp, identifiable emotion. The viewer laughs out loud, gets angry, feels seen, or experiences awe. The emotion is the primary experience of watching.
  - Example (laughter): A comedy clip where the punchline lands so hard the viewer laughs before processing the words.
  - Example (validation): A motivation clip that articulates something the viewer has felt but couldn't express — they feel "yes, exactly."

- **8:** Strong emotional response, but secondary to the intellectual content. The viewer feels something, but the clip is primarily delivering an insight.
  - Example: A business clip with a contrarian take that produces mild anger in viewers who disagree and mild validation in viewers who agree.

- **6:** Moderate emotional response. The clip is engaging but not viscerally moving.
  - Example: An educational clip that produces mild curiosity but no stronger emotion.

- **4:** Low emotional response. The clip is informative but flat.
  - Example: A business clip with a useful but dry insight.

- **2:** Minimal emotion. The clip is essentially informational with no emotional charge.
  - Example: A technical explainer with no stakes or surprise.

- **0:** No emotional response. The clip is dead — often a platitude, a recitation of facts, or a meandering discussion with no charge.

**Common scoring errors:**
- Inflating "interesting" as an emotion. "Interesting" is not an emotion — it's a cognitive assessment. Score based on visceral response, not intellectual engagement.
- Inflating because the speaker is emotional. The speaker's emotion doesn't transfer if the writing doesn't carry it. A speaker crying over a platitude still produces no emotion in the viewer.
- Confusing intensity with quality. Anger and laughter are both emotions; one isn't better than the other. Score whether the emotion is produced, not whether it's "positive."

## 5. Share Trigger

**Definition:** Does the clip express an identity, belief, or value the viewer will want to publicly endorse by sharing?

**Why it matters:** Shares drive reach more than views. A clip with high view-to-share ratio gets algorithmic promotion; a clip with low view-to-share ratio gets buried. Share triggers are what turn views into reach.

**Anchor descriptors:**

- **10 (max):** The clip contains a clear, articulable position or value that viewers will want to publicly endorse. The position is specific enough to be a real identity signal (not "believe in yourself" — too vague) but broad enough to appeal to a meaningful audience (not a niche in-joke).
  - Example: "Discipline is just self-respect at scale." — viewers who identify as disciplined share this to signal they're in the discipline-as-identity tribe.
  - Example: "If your company isn't paying you enough to live within five miles of the office, you don't have a job — they have a hostage." — remote work advocates share this as an identity signal.

- **8:** Strong share trigger but slightly less universal. The position resonates with a specific tribe (entrepreneurs, parents, remote workers) but not beyond.
  - Example: A clip specifically about the founder experience that founders will share but employees won't.

- **6:** Moderate share trigger. The clip is shareable but not as an identity signal — more as "this is interesting."
  - Example: An educational clip with a surprising fact that viewers share to look smart, but not to signal identity.

- **4:** Weak share trigger. The clip might be shared occasionally but not as a deliberate identity expression.
  - Example: A useful tutorial clip that gets saved more than shared.

- **2:** Minimal share trigger. The clip is consumed and forgotten.
  - Example: A clip with a generic insight that doesn't take a position.

- **0:** No share trigger. The clip is purely informational or purely entertaining with no position to endorse.
  - Example: "Today's weather is sunny."

**Common scoring errors:**
- Inflating because the clip is "interesting." Interest drives saves, not shares. Saves are good but shares drive reach.
- Confusing controversy with share trigger. Controversial clips get engagement but not necessarily shares — viewers may agree privately but not want to publicly endorse.
- Inflating because the position is extreme. Extreme positions get fewer shares, not more, because viewers won't publicly endorse them. The sweet spot is "specific enough to be a real signal, broad enough to be safe to share."

## 6. Replay Value

**Definition:** Is the clip worth rewatching? Does it have density, nuance, or a joke that benefits from re-hearing?

**Why it matters:** Rewatches are a strong algorithmic signal. Platforms promote clips with high rewatch rates because they indicate the content rewards attention. Replay value is also what turns a one-time view into a memorable clip — the viewer internalizes it.

**Anchor descriptors:**

- **10 (max):** The clip clearly rewards rewatching. Either because it's too dense to absorb in one pass, because the joke has layers that surface on rewatch, or because the emotional payoff hits harder the second time.
  - Example: A high-density business clip where the viewer needs to rewatch to absorb all the tactics.
  - Example: A comedy bit with a callback that's only funny on rewatch once you know the punchline.

- **8:** Strong rewatch value. The clip has layers or density that benefit from a second viewing, but the first viewing is satisfying on its own.
  - Example: A clip with a clear insight plus a subtle secondary observation that viewers catch on rewatch.

- **6:** Moderate rewatch value. The clip is enjoyable once but doesn't demand rewatching. Some viewers will rewatch; most won't.

- **4:** Low rewatch value. The clip is satisfying once but fully absorbed in a single viewing. No reason to rewatch.
  - Example: A clear, simple tutorial clip.

- **2:** Minimal rewatch value. The clip is essentially disposable — watched once, forgotten.

- **0:** Negative rewatch value. The clip is worse on rewatch — often because the surprise is gone (a one-time reveal) or because the flaws are more visible the second time.

**Common scoring errors:**
- Inflating because the clip is good. Good clips don't necessarily have replay value. A clean, simple insight can be a 10/10 clip with low replay value.
- Confusing length with replay value. Longer clips aren't more replayable — often less, because the time investment to rewatch is higher.
- Deflating because the clip is short. Short dense clips have the highest replay value — they're cheap to rewatch.

## 7. Cut Quality

**Definition:** Is there a clean in-point and out-point? Does the clip start mid-sentence or end abruptly? Are there awkward pauses, crosstalk, or audio issues that would require editing?

**Why it matters:** Even great content fails if the cut is bad. A clip that starts mid-sentence feels amateurish; a clip that ends mid-thought leaves the viewer unsatisfied. Cut quality is the difference between a clip that feels produced and a clip that feels scraped.

**Anchor descriptors:**

- **10 (max):** The clip has a clean sentence-level in-point and out-point. The first word is the start of a complete thought; the last word is the end of one. No awkward pauses, no crosstalk, no audio issues. Could be posted as-is.
  - Example: A clip that starts on "Here's the thing nobody tells you about..." and ends on "...and that's the whole game." Both natural sentence boundaries.

- **8:** Mostly clean cut with minor issues. One small awkward pause, or an in-point that's slightly before a complete thought but recoverable with a 1-second trim.
  - Example: A clip that starts with "Um, so..." — needs a 1-second trim to be clean.

- **6:** Moderate cut issues. The in-point or out-point is mid-thought and requires 2–3 seconds of trimming. Or there's crosstalk that needs to be edited out.
  - Example: A clip that starts mid-sentence and needs the first 2 seconds cut.

- **4:** Significant cut issues. The clip requires substantial editing — multiple trims, crosstalk removal, or audio cleanup.
  - Example: A clip where the host interrupts the guest twice, requiring surgical editing.

- **2:** Poor cut quality. The clip is essentially uncuttable without destroying the content. Often because the speaker rambles, interrupts themselves, or the audio is degraded.

- **0:** Uncuttable. The clip can't be salvaged — the speaker is incoherent, the audio is broken, or the content is too fragmented.

**Common scoring errors:**
- Inflating because the content is good. Cut quality is independent of content quality. A great clip with a bad cut is a Tier B, not a Tier A.
- Deflating because the clip "needs editing." Most clips need *some* editing. Score based on whether the editing is trivial (8–10), moderate (5–7), or significant (0–4).
- Ignoring the out-point. The out-point matters as much as the in-point. A clip that ends mid-sentence feels worse than one that starts mid-sentence.

## 8. Cross-Platform Fit

**Definition:** Does the clip fit the 15–30 second constraint for TikTok/Reels/Shorts without being gutted? Will it work across all four target platforms, or only some?

**Why it matters:** The user is cutting clips for short-form distribution. A clip that doesn't fit the format constraints is useless regardless of its other qualities. Cross-platform fit is the practical axis — it gates whether the clip is actually usable.

**Anchor descriptors:**

- **15 (max):** The clip is naturally 15–30 seconds and works on all four target platforms (TikTok, Shorts, Reels, X). No major adaptation needed. The content fits each platform's audience expectations.
  - Example: A 22-second clip with a strong hook, dense payoff, and shareable take that works equally well on TikTok (entertainment-forward), Shorts (search-friendly), Reels (aesthetic-aware), and X (quote-tweetable).

- **12:** The clip fits the length constraint and works on 3 of 4 platforms. One platform is a weaker fit, usually because of tone or content style.
  - Example: A clip that's perfect for TikTok/Reels/Shorts but slightly too casual for X.

- **10:** The clip fits the length constraint and works on 2 of 4 platforms. Multiple platforms are weaker fits.
  - Example: A lifestyle clip that works on TikTok/Reels but not on Shorts/X.

- **7:** The clip is slightly over length (30–40 seconds) and requires trimming to fit. After trimming, works on most platforms but loses some density.
  - Example: A 35-second clip that needs to be cut to 28 seconds, losing one of its insights.

- **5:** The clip is significantly over length (40–60 seconds) or only works on one platform. Substantial editing required.
  - Example: A 50-second clip that needs to be cut to 30 seconds, losing significant content.

- **2:** The clip doesn't fit the format. Either way too long (60+ seconds) or the content is fundamentally unsuitable for short-form.
  - Example: A 90-second explanation that can't be compressed without losing the point.

- **0:** The clip is unusable for short-form. Either a long-form segment that can't be cut, or content that requires visual/context that short-form can't provide.

**Common scoring errors:**
- Inflating because the clip is "good enough" for one platform. Cross-platform fit requires working on *multiple* platforms, not just one.
- Deflating because the clip "doesn't fit X." X has the most distinct audience of the four. If a clip works on TikTok/Shorts/Reels, score at least 10 even if X is a weak fit.
- Confusing length with fit. Length is one factor; tone, style, and audience match also matter.

## Scoring discipline

The rubric only works if applied honestly. A few discipline principles:

1. **Score each axis independently.** Don't let a strong Hook score influence your Payoff Density score. Each axis measures a distinct quality.

2. **Anchor on the descriptors.** When in doubt, reread the anchor descriptors and find the closest match. Don't score based on feeling — score based on the rubric.

3. **Don't inflate to make the user feel good.** The skill's value is being a reliable filter. A clip that's a 62 should be scored 62, not 75. Inflation destroys the user's trust over time.

4. **Don't deflate to seem rigorous.** A great clip is a great clip. Score it appropriately. False humility is as bad as false generosity.

5. **When borderline, round to the lower score.** Between a 7 and an 8 on Hook Strength? Score 7. The user can always override; the skill's role is to be the conservative filter.

6. **The total score is the sum, not a vibe.** Don't decide on a total and then back-fill the axis scores. Score each axis, sum them, and accept the result.

7. **Anti-patterns show up in the scores.** If a clip trips an anti-pattern, expect to see it reflected in the relevant axis. If the score doesn't reflect the anti-pattern, either the anti-pattern call was wrong or the scoring was inflated. Reconcile.

## 9. Narrative Arc (Tier L only, max 15)

**Definition:** Does the clip tell a complete story with setup, rising tension, climax, and resolution? Only applies to Tier L (long-form, 60–180s).

**Why it matters:** Long-form clips survive on narrative structure, not on individual punchlines. A 90-second clip without a clear arc feels like "60 seconds of a person talking" — even if every sentence is interesting, the viewer loses the thread and drops off. The arc is what makes long-form watchable. This is the defining axis for Tier L; without it, the other axes don't matter.

**Tier L anchor descriptors:**

- **15 (max):** The clip has a crystal-clear arc with all four elements:
  - **Setup** (0–20% of the clip): establishes where we are, who's involved, what's at stake
  - **Rising tension** (20–60%): something happens that escalates the stakes
  - **Climax** (60–80%): the moment of peak tension or insight — the "turn"
  - **Resolution** (80–100%): what changed, what was learned, what's next

  Example: A founder's pivot story — "We were at $4M ARR, growing 20% MoM, and I decided to pivot the entire product. [Setup] I fired our top customer. The board wanted to fire me. Two co-founders quit. Investors stopped returning calls for three weeks. [Rising tension] I cried every day. I gained 15 pounds. I stopped sleeping. [Climax] Six months later, the new product launched, and we went from $4M to $40M in 18 months. The pivot was right. The leadership was wrong. [Resolution]"

- **12:** Strong arc with all four elements, but one element is slightly underdeveloped. Often the setup is too brief or the resolution is too abrupt.

- **10:** Has an arc but it's loose. The viewer can follow the story but the structure isn't tight. Often the rising tension section meanders.

- **7:** Weak arc. The clip has multiple beats but they don't clearly escalate. Feels like "and then this happened, and then this happened" rather than a story.

- **5:** No clear arc. The clip is just a sequence of related thoughts. The viewer can follow each sentence but doesn't feel a story unfolding.

- **2:** Anti-arc. The clip starts somewhere, goes nowhere, and ends without resolution. Often a tangent or digression that never returns to the main thread.

- **0:** The clip is incoherent. No narrative structure, no clear topic, no resolution. Often a fragment of a longer story that doesn't stand alone.

**Common scoring errors:**
- **Inflating because the content is interesting.** Interesting content without structure is still a low Narrative Arc score. The arc is about structure, not content quality.
- **Inflating because the speaker is charismatic.** Charisma carries attention but doesn't create an arc.
- **Deflating because the arc is subtle.** Some stories have quiet arcs — the tension is internal, the climax is a realization rather than an event. Subtle arcs can still score 12+ if the structure is there.
- **Confusing density with arc.** A clip can be dense (many ideas per second) without having an arc. Arc is about sequence and escalation, not quantity.
- **Not checking for the resolution.** Many clips have setup + tension + climax but no resolution. The clip ends on the climax without telling the viewer what changed. Score these 10 max — the resolution is what makes the arc satisfying.

### Tier L worked example

**Input (90 seconds):**
> [00:14:30] Guest: "So I want to tell you about the worst day of my career. [Setup] It was March 2019. I'd been CEO for three years. We'd just raised our Series B — $20M at a $80M post. The next morning, I got a call from our biggest customer. They were leaving. [Rising tension] Not because of price. Not because of product.
